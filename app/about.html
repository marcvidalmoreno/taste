Why

I know what I like when I see it. I could never say it in words.

Over the years I saved about a thousand wallpapers. Each one was a small decision: a second of looking, a yes. Together they are a record of my taste, kept by my eyes and never written down. Not even I could read it.

That is how taste works for most of us. Recognising is fast and sure; describing is slow and vague. The brain gets the gist of a picture in a fraction of a second, long before we find a word for it. Ask me to describe the light I love and I'll give you an adjective. Show me two images and I'll pick one at once.

An image model works the other way round. It can't look at my folder, but it reads words very well. So this project translates: from a folder of images I chose to written styles, with a palette, a light, a composition, a medium, a mood and, just as important, what to avoid.

Then it closes the loop. Every image made from those words comes back to me, and my reaction, a thumb and a few words, goes back into them. The description stops being a guess I wrote once and becomes something that learns from what I actually choose.

What it believes

Taste grows from evidence
What I say I like and what I keep are not always the same. So the styles change from my reactions to real images, not from what I think I want. Downs teach as much as ups.
Recognition first, words second
I'm asked to judge, not to describe. A thumb takes a second; a "why" is optional. The words are the machine's job: it turns my choices into language and shows me the result.
The human decides, the machine runs
I always stand on the side of the human, and here that human is me. Codex makes and drafts, Claude organises, and nothing changes in a style until I press Apply. Every change shows what it was before and can be undone.
Nothing hidden
Trust should match reality. When a thumb steers the next runs, the page says which parts it moved. When a run spends allowance, the cost is in view before I press Start. I can always see why the machine did what it did.
Technology out of sight
I give feedback where I feel it: on the image, on the style, in the moment. No scripts, no files to edit, no chat to remember. The machinery runs quietly behind the page.
One change, one lesson
A variation changes one thing at a time. If palette and subject change together, I can't tell which one I liked. Small steps make the feedback mean something.

What it is not

It is not a way to copy anyone. The wallpapers belong to the people who made them. They stay on my laptop, and the styles describe qualities (a light, a palette, a way of framing) rather than reproduce works. Only the descriptions and the images I make are mine to show.

It is not a product either. It is a small, real place where I learn how people and AI can work together: where the machine helps, where it must ask, and how a calm interface keeps a person in control.

How it grew

  1. 8 Nov 2026 A first try: styles found by grouping the wallpapers by colour. Quick, and wrong two times out of three.
  2. 13 Nov 2026 Rebuilt by eye: every wallpaper tagged for subject, medium, mood and light, and each style approved one by one. Images made through Codex on my own plan, and this web app to rate them.
  3. 14–19 Nov 2026 More collections, more styles, a few dropped again after testing, and more variety inside each style.
  4. 18 Nov 2026 Rating became a simple thumb up or down, with an optional why.
  5. 21 Nov 2026 The app became the interface: runs, remixes, style changes and suggestions all happen in the page.

Who does what

  • Me: I look, react and decide. Every change to a style needs my OK.
  • Codex: makes the images, one at a time, and drafts words for me to read: new subjects, remix prompts, style suggestions. It never changes a style on its own.
  • Claude: looks after the styles, reads my notes, builds the tools and this app, and keeps a record of every change with a way to undo it.
